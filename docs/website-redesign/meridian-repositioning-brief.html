Meridian Repositioning Brief

How to turn a site that says "we build AI agents" into one where a non-technical business owner thinks: I understand what this is, I can see how it helps my business, and I know what to do next.

Source note: the live site could not be fetched from the sandbox used to write this brief, so the "today" analysis is reconstructed from search-engine caches of the homepage and the audit blog post. Anything that depends on a detail those caches did not contain is called out in Unresolved Questions.

The verdict

The site has the right raw materials and the wrong frame. The real offer is a practitioner who finds a business's repetitive work and builds AI that does it inside the tools they already use, with a paid audit as the on-ramp. What the site leads with instead is vendor vocabulary: "AI implementation studio", "agents", "knowledge base", "virtual employee". Four nouns for one service, and none of them is the customer's noun.

Three changes carry most of the value.

  1. Lead with the work, not the technology. The headline names what gets handed off (quotes, follow-ups, data entry, reporting), the tools it happens in, and how long it takes. "Agents" and "virtual employees" become supporting language, or go.
  2. Make the ladder visible: Find, Build, Run. Free 30-minute fit call → Workflow Audit ($2,500, 2 weeks) → Workflow Build (fixed price, one workflow at a time) → optional Care. Today the audit lives only in a blog post and the price is hidden.
  3. Repair the only conversion path. The contact form is broken, so the site's lead rate is effectively zero regardless of copy. Replace it with a calendar booking plus a three-field form and a visible email address.

The memorable phrase to build the brand around: The repetitive work, handled. The structural phrase: One workflow at a time.

A brand problem worth naming

"Meridian" is crowded in AI: a funded agentic-spreadsheet startup, a private-equity CRM, an accounting AI product, and a "Meridian Flow Inc" doing AI e-commerce automation. "Meridian flow" itself is a Traditional Chinese Medicine term, so search for the name returns acupuncture clinics. Brand search will not find you. This brief does not recommend renaming now. It does recommend that the founder's name and the phrase "AI workflows for small business, Montréal" carry the findability, and that the rename question be decided deliberately (see Unresolved Questions).

DecisionDemote "virtual employee" from primary framing to an optional aside in the Build tier. Why the ladder converts better than "book a discovery call". A non-technical owner cannot evaluate "AI agents". They can evaluate "$2,500, two weeks, a roadmap I keep". The audit is a small, reversible decision that builds the trust the build needs. Crediting the audit toward the build removes the "am I paying twice" objection and pulls the buyer forward.

Guarantees to consider

  • Audit: "If the audit finds nothing worth building, we say so, and you keep the roadmap." Cheap to offer; you would say it anyway.
  • Build: "We keep tuning until it does what we agreed in the spec, or the last milestone is free." Confirms fixed scope means fixed scope. Confirm you are comfortable with this before publishing.

Homepage section order

The order follows how an owner decides: recognise the problem, see that the solution is concrete, see the path and the price, check the people, clear objections, act.

  1. Hero. Outcome headline, tools named, time frame, primary CTA, "no pitch" microcopy, location line. Signature visual: a team's Tuesday-morning task list with rows highlighted "handed off".
  2. Sound familiar. Three pain vignettes in the owner's language. Ends with the ChatGPT-didn't-stick line that names your actual job: the wiring.
  3. What we build. Six concrete workflows, each Before → After → Tools. This is the section that makes AI legible to a non-technical reader.
  4. How it works. Find, Build, Run with duration and price on each step. The numbering is honest because it is a sequence.
  5. Proof. MP Cutting Tools: the quote plus three qualitative outcomes (no figures), then "Who you'll work with" (founder).
  6. Why Meridian. The six differentiators, one line each.
  7. Pricing. The ladder again as three columns. Transparency is a trust signal for this buyer.
  8. FAQ. Eight objections, answered plainly.
  9. Book. Calendar embed, fallback form, email, phone.
  10. Footer. Montréal, FR/EN, email, LinkedIn, privacy.

Navigation: How it works · Examples · Pricing · About · Blog, plus a persistent "Book a free fit call" button. Five links, all anchoring into the homepage except About and Blog.

Conversion fixes

The contact experience

  • Fix or replace the form this week. If the site is static, wire the form to a hosted form endpoint (Formspree, Basin, Netlify Forms) and test it from a phone. If it is on a site builder, reconnect the notification email. Either way, add a plain-text email address next to it so a broken form is never a dead end again.
  • Make booking the primary action. Embed a free scheduler (Cal.com or Calendly) with a 30-minute "Fit call" event. Booking a slot is lower-effort than composing a message and gives you a committed time.
  • Three fields, no more. Name, work email, one text box: "What's eating your team's time?" Every extra field costs roughly 7% of submissions. The text box doubles as qualification.
  • Confirm loudly. Success state on the page ("Got it. You'll hear from Matt within one business day.") and an auto-reply from a human address, not noreply.
  • Put the CTA everywhere a decision happens. Nav, hero, after Examples, after Pricing, after FAQ, and the final block. Same wording each time.

Trust signals that cost nothing

  • Matt's name, photo, and a line about his daily AI work on the homepage.
  • Concrete outcomes from MP Cutting Tools stated in words, with their permission: what is done before the rep sits down, what the rep still does. Specific verbs carry the proof when figures are off the table.
  • Pricing on the page. Owners assume hidden prices are high prices.
  • "Which tools" list with logos they recognise.
  • A one-page redacted roadmap sample on /audit so they can see what $2,500 buys.

A second path for the not-ready visitor

Most visitors will not book on the first visit. Give them one lightweight thing to take: a one-page checklist, "Twelve tasks worth automating first in a small business", in exchange for an email. Keep it out of the hero; place it after Examples and in the footer. Phase 2, after the primary path works.

Test hypotheses once traffic exists

  1. If the hero headline names the work ("Hand off the work your team keeps redoing") instead of the technology, fit-call bookings will rise, because the reader recognises their own problem in the first line.
  2. If pricing is on the homepage, booking rate will rise even if some visitors leave, because the ones who book will be qualified and the total lead count will not fall.
  3. If the primary CTA is "Pick a time" with a calendar instead of a form, completed leads will rise, because scheduling asks for less writing than a message.

Interaction brief

  • Primary flow: land → recognise pain (Sound familiar) → see concrete work (Examples) → see path and price → check the person → clear objections → pick a time. Every section ends within one scroll of a CTA.
  • Nav: anchors scroll smoothly; the booking button stays visible on scroll. On mobile, a bottom sticky bar carries "Book a free fit call".
  • Form states: inline validation on blur; specific messages ("Enter a work email so we can reply"); submit button reads "Send" then "Sending…" then success text in place. Never clear the form on error.
  • FAQ: disclosure widgets, keyboard operable, first item open by default so the section is never an empty list of questions.
  • Motion: the hero task list strikes through and highlights three rows once, on load, over about 1.6 seconds. Everything else is hover and focus only. Respect prefers-reduced-motion.
  • Empty and error states: if the calendar embed fails to load, the fallback form and email are already visible beneath it, so the page never shows a blank box.
  • Accessibility: 4.5:1 contrast on all text, visible focus rings, headings in order, buttons that say what happens, form labels always visible.
  • Verification: submit the form from a phone and a laptop; book a test slot; confirm both emails arrive; run Lighthouse and keep mobile load under three seconds; check French toggle if shipped.

Traffic: the site can't convert visitors it doesn't get

The rewrite fixes conversion. Traffic needs three channels that fit a solo practitioner and cost time, not money.

  • Founder-led LinkedIn. Two posts a week showing one real workflow each: the before, the after, one screenshot. The homepage Examples section is the content calendar. Link to /work, not the homepage.
  • Local search. A Google Business Profile for Montréal, and a homepage that says "Montréal" and "automatisation IA" in real sentences. "AI automation Montreal" is a query owners actually type and the competition is thin.
  • Guides that answer a tool-specific question. "How to draft quotes from Outlook emails with AI", "Automating supplier invoices into QuickBooks". Each ends with the audit. The existing audit post is the model; it just needs siblings.

Referrals from the first two or three clients will outperform all of this, which is one more reason to get their stories on the page.
